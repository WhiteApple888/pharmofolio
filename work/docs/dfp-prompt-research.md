# Development Framework for Pharmacists: source notes

Primary source: Ministry of Health, Singapore, [Development Framework for Pharmacists, Continuum of Competency, version 2.0](<../../development-framework-for-pharmacists-2024-v2-0 1.pdf>). References below are **PDF page numbers** (which match the printed page numbers). The first version is May 2020 and this version is June 2024 (p. 2). No general effective date or expiration date is stated on that page.

## Interpretation and assessment

- The framework has 26 standards in seven domains. Domains 1–6 extend the advanced-practice framework to foundation; Domain 7 concerns professionalism and is outside the present domain list (p. 6).
- The **performance criterion** states the level and quality of activity used to evaluate competence (p. 13). The **Evidence** column describes the abilities or other evidence demonstrating that standard; **Evidence Examples** are concrete documents, data or information illustrating it (p. 12). Read the criterion alongside the corresponding evidence, rather than treating a document title alone as proof (pp. 9–10, 19).
- The page 7 performance levels are: **Foundation** — completes tasks with help, focusing on acquiring knowledge or skill; **Intermediate** — completes tasks with occasional help, applying and enhancing knowledge or skill; **Advanced** — routinely manages complex situations and is a recognised organisational resource, with broad organisational or professional focus; **Expert** — recognised authority with breadth of experience, links issues to broader organisational concerns, creates applications and processes, and focuses on strategy (p. 7). These are proficiency levels, not numerical grades; assess the relevant standard and demonstrated scope rather than a job title alone.
- For Foundation, gather evidence such as case or intervention logs and workplace-based assessments, then seek a second opinion from a peer or coach/mentor familiar with the work (p. 9). The FAQ says portfolio assessment is not recommended at this stage and suggests entrustable professional activities to demonstrate competence (p. 16).
- For Intermediate, Advanced and Expert, gather evidence from the **last three years**, or the **last five years for the research domain**; identify the level that best describes current practice and seek a second opinion if needed (p. 10). These windows are general instructions for those levels, not an instruction to reject an undated item without clarification or to impose a Foundation portfolio window. Ask for the activity date and assessment date when currency is unclear. Individual research evidence examples also specify their own periods, including three-year examples for standards 6.1 and 6.2 and a five-year example for 6.3 (pp. 60, 62–63).
- Evidence examples are **non-exhaustive** (pp. 9–10). Quality and adequacy matter more than the number of documents; seek contextual detail about depth of practice, and the same example may support several competencies (pp. 17, 19). Do not invent activities, outcomes, dates, independence, authorship, or level. Insufficient detail calls for clarification rather than a confident rating.
- Appendix B's public-healthcare institutional guidance associates **PH03** with at least 85% of standards at Intermediate or above; **PH04** with at least 70% at Advanced or above; **PH05** with at least 80% at Advanced or above, with 20% of standards at Expert; and **PH06** with at least 40% at Expert (p. 69). These are portfolio-level job-grade benchmarks, not automatic thresholds for rating one item. Appendix B describes Intermediate as completing tasks independently with occasional expert assistance, whereas the general description on p. 7 says occasional help; preserve the source context rather than merging them into a new rule.

## Domains 1–6: standards, criteria and illustrative evidence

Each entry points to the actual level-specific performance criteria, evidence and examples; these summaries are orientation, not substitutes for reading the applicable rows.

### 1. Expert Professional Practice (overview p. 21)

- **1.1 Skills and knowledge** (pp. 22–23): apply pharmaceutical knowledge safely, progressing to specialist expertise and leading practice/research. Examples include workplace assessments, documented interventions or enquiries and translational research.
- **1.2 Patient care / professional delivery** (pp. 24–25): accountability progresses from safe, effective care for individual patients to services, defined groups and wider service development. Examples include case logs, interventions, service indicators, improvement cycles and guidelines.
- **1.3 Reasoning and judgement** (pp. 26–27): apply policies and analyse problems, progressing to timely decisions in complex or uncertain situations. Examples include feedback, governance contributions and guideline development.
- **1.4 Professional autonomy** (p. 28): work within legal and ethical requirements, progressing to risk-based decisions and action in ambiguous contexts. Examples include workplace feedback, service initiation and practice guidelines.

### 2. Building Working Relationships (overview p. 29)

- **2.1 Effective communication** (pp. 30–32): tailor communication for stakeholders, progressing through complex situations to conveying ideas at management level. Examples include observational and 360-degree feedback and collaboration evidence.
- **2.2 Collaboration and consultation** (pp. 33–34): respect and engage others, contribute to teams, and progressively lead partnerships across teams or institutions. Examples include feedback and workgroup, committee or quality-improvement contributions.

### 3. Leadership (overview p. 35)

- **3.1 Visioning** (pp. 36–37): align work with organisational purpose, progressing to creating and influencing others towards a vision. Examples include aligned goals, strategies and stakeholder feedback.
- **3.2 Strategic planning** (pp. 38–40): apply policy and situational awareness, progressing to organisational strategy and wider influence. Examples include policy explanations, implementation and national workgroup contributions.
- **3.3 Innovation** (p. 41): identify service gaps and contribute ideas, progressing to leading substantial improvement and anticipating trends. Examples include improvement suggestions, quality-improvement projects and research.
- **3.4 Motivating individuals** (pp. 42–43): develop self-awareness and support peers, progressing to motivating teams and others more widely. Examples include reflection, peer support and achievement of team or departmental goals.

### 4. Management (overview p. 44)

- **4.1 Implementing organisational priorities** (p. 45): support departmental priorities, progressing to formulating and leading strategies; examples include suggestions, plans and team results.
- **4.2 Resource utilisation** (pp. 46–47): use resources responsibly and progressively manage or optimise staffing, inventory, budgets and projects; examples include rosters, inventory management and budget plans.
- **4.3 Standards of practice** (p. 48): apply and improve guidelines, progressing to setting and monitoring standards; examples include SOP recommendations and updated or new protocols.
- **4.4 Managing risk** (p. 49): understand and communicate risks, comply with procedures, and progressively develop or revise risk controls; examples include error prevention and revised work procedures.
- **4.5 Managing performance** (pp. 50–51): understand and review one's own performance, progressing to assessing and developing team or departmental performance; examples include development plans, documented feedback and departmental results.
- **4.6 Managing change** (p. 52): understand and support change, progressing to planning, implementing and leading change; examples include stakeholder feedback, implementation activities and performance indicators.

### 5. Education, Training and Development (overview p. 53)

- **5.1 Developing self and others** (pp. 54–55): lifelong learning and self-development progress to coaching, mentoring and developing others. Examples include CPD reviews, feedback and documented mentoring contributions.
- **5.2 Conducting education and training** (pp. 56–57): share knowledge and support training, progressing to assessing trainees and leading education programmes. Examples include presentations, documented assessment feedback and programme leadership.

### 6. Research and Evaluation (overview p. 58)

- **6.1 Critical evaluation and identifying gaps** (pp. 59–60): evaluate literature and data, progressing to influencing departmental practice and identifying major gaps or influencing practice nationally/internationally. Examples include journal clubs, guideline work, peer review and publications.
- **6.2 Developing and evaluating research protocols** (pp. 61–62): understand research governance and methods, progressing to leading ethical research and critically appraising protocols or writing grants. Examples include journal-club work, investigator roles, authorised protocols and review-board service.
- **6.3 Disseminating evidence** (p. 63): participate under guidance, progress to communicating findings and publishing, and at Expert demonstrate ongoing anchor authorship. Examples include abstracts, presentations and publications. The Expert example mentions at least 10 publications in the last five years; it is an example in the source, not a universal stand-alone rule for all research competencies.
- **6.4 Guiding research or projects** (p. 64): awareness of departmental research progresses to guidance, supervised research with experts, and independent postgraduate supervision. Examples include supervising projects and mentoring grant applicants; the Foundation row has no listed example.
- **6.5 Establishing partnerships** (p. 65): contribute to a team, develop collaborator networks, and progressively coordinate multidisciplinary research. Examples include project contributions, correspondence and principal-investigator roles.
